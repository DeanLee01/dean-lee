Every financial asset prices against an unobservable anchor: the natural rate of interest, commonly denoted as r-star. It is the real interest rate that balances savings and investment when output is running at potential and inflation remains stable. For more than two decades following the 2001 dot-com crash and the 2008 financial crisis, central bankers and market participants operated under a secular stagnation consensus. Aging demographics, abundant global savings, and low capital intensity across mature economies pushed equilibrium rates toward zero.

The current capital expenditure wave across artificial intelligence challenges that baseline. Hyperscalers and infrastructure funds are deploying hundreds of billions of dollars into data centers, power generation, and accelerated computing hardware. If computing infrastructure becomes the dominant capital good of the corporate sector, that physical accumulation directly affects the real cost of capital.

In a paper published in the Journal of Monetary Economics titled "From servers to rates: AI, ICT capital, and the natural rate" (doi:10.1016/j.jmoneco.2026.104016), economists Giovanni Melina and Stefania Villa evaluate this mechanism directly. Extending a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium model based on the Smets and Wouters framework, the authors separate physical capital into two categories: traditional non-ICT capital, such as industrial machinery and commercial structures, and ICT capital, including servers, datacenter facilities, networking hardware, and software. Estimating the model with Bayesian methods on quarterly United States macroeconomic data, they trace how investment-specific technology shocks transmit to the natural rate.

Their model yields several clear dynamics. When a technology shock improves the efficiency of transforming investment dollars into productive computing equipment, it initially operates like an investment demand shock rather than a supply expansion. In the early phase of the capital cycle, households smooth consumption while capital goods producers redirect resources into hardware production. The equilibrium natural rate softens briefly during the initial installation window, then rises persistently as the accumulated capital stock becomes operational and raises the expected marginal product of capital.

The interaction between computing capital and human labor shapes the magnitude of this shift. Econometric estimation in the paper indicates that ICT capital and labor have historically acted as gross substitutes in the aggregate United States economy, with their elasticity of substitution drifting upward over time. When capital and labor are substitutes, installing additional compute allows firms to displace labor expenses and increase capital share. Because firms face high returns on expanding that capital base, aggregate demand for financing remains sustained.

Melina and Villa examine two distinct trajectories for the current technological cycle. Under a baseline continuation of historical ICT deepening, where investment grows along its established historical trend, the impact on the natural rate is modest, lifting equilibrium real rates by only a few basis points over several years. Under an ambitious scenario, where generative models produce both a persistent acceleration in total factor productivity and an upward structural shift in capital-labor substitutability, the natural rate rises substantially and remains elevated for years.

This scenario exposes a valuation tension for the technology sector financing the buildout. Hyperscalers and venture funds justify current hardware purchases by projecting extraordinary multi-decade cash flows. However, financial valuation discounts those distant cash flows against the risk-free rate. If the mass deployment of server infrastructure succeeds in driving up the natural rate, the equilibrium discount rate across the economy rises alongside it.

For companies whose equity valuations depend heavily on earnings projected ten or fifteen years ahead, a higher equilibrium rate applies continuous pressure to valuation multiples. The capital expenditure unleashed to secure computing scale lifts the macroeconomic hurdle rate that discounts the resulting profits.

Financial markets often treat the cost of capital as an administrative decision determined by central bank committees responding to monthly inflation prints. The structural finding from Melina and Villa shows that the physical investment cycle itself can reset the underlying anchor. When an industry sets out to rebuild global computing infrastructure, it bids aggressively for aggregate savings. If that capital wave delivers on its promised productivity gains, the low-rate regime that subsidized its early stages will not return.
