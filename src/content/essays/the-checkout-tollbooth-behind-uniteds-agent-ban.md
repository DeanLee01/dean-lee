On November 11, 2026, United Airlines updated its [Contract of Carriage](https://www.united.com/en/us/fly/contract-of-carriage.html) to prohibit personal AI assistants and AI-enabled browsers from booking, modifying, or servicing tickets. Under the revised Rule 1 definition, an AI agent is covered whether or not the passenger supervises or approves its actions, and regardless of whether the software reaches United directly or through a travel agency. Rule 5.H lets United cancel agent-handled reservations without notice, Rule 6.J.6 places AI agents alongside hidden-city and throwaway ticketing under prohibited practices, and Rule 6.K authorizes ticket invalidation, fare-difference recovery, and MileagePlus account termination.

United's public compliance and security rationale has real operational weight. Airlines face strict liability under federal hazardous-materials rules ([49 CFR 175.25](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/united-airlines-bans-ai-agents-booking-flights/)) that require passengers to receive and acknowledge dangerous-goods notices at ticketing and check-in. Handing MileagePlus credentials and stored payment methods to unvetted third-party browser tools also creates genuine dispute and fraud exposure when an assistant buys the wrong fare class or cancels a return leg without clear legal accountability. Unlike accredited travel agencies bound by airline distribution contracts and debit-memo settlement rules, consumer-side browser agents currently sit outside every carrier's commercial liability perimeter.

The commercial incentive runs deeper than compliance. United CEO Scott Kirby told [*The Wall Street Journal*](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/article/bots-dont-fly-here-united-airlines-restricts-ai-agents-from-booking-flights-203943442.html) that he did not want "to create another middleman." Airlines spent two decades pushing travelers onto direct websites and mobile apps to reduce global distribution system fees and control how fares appear. An AI assistant that sits between the traveler and the carrier reintroduces an intermediary at the top of the funnel, one that can eventually rank carriers, charge referral fees, or steer demand toward airlines that pay for placement.

Two unit-economic pressures explain why United moved before agent booking volume even became large. First is server query economics. Legacy reservation infrastructure was priced around human search friction, where a traveler compares a dozen itineraries before stopping. Automated agents lack a natural stopping point. As [Travelers Today reported](https://www.travelerstoday.com/articles/60301/20260624/ai-travel-search-breaking-airline-systems-amadeus-bets-precomputed-fares.htm) earlier this year, one coding agent pulled 881,076 fare combinations from Etihad Airways in a single search, and PhocusWire estimated that AI-driven look-to-book ratios can exceed 200,000 queries per completed ticket. Because IATA's New Distribution Capability replaced static 26-bucket fare filings with live continuous pricing calculations, every automated permutation forces real compute work on airline systems. United already charges agencies penalty fees for excess NDC search traffic, and blocking unauthorized agent transactions keeps that query flood away from its direct checkout pipeline.

Second is margin protection inside airline price discrimination. In Paul Samuelson and William Nordhaus's textbook formulation in *Economics* (Chapter 10, "Price Discrimination"), airlines are the canonical example of firms segmenting price-inelastic business travelers from elastic leisure buyers by pairing bare-bones base tiers with paid seat selection, baggage fees, and fare fences. United's checkout funnel relies on persuading travelers to upgrade out of Basic Economy or pay for ancillaries in the booking flow. A well-calibrated buyer-side agent strips away visual nudges, cross-references the traveler's co-branded credit card and elite status waivers automatically, and declines add-ons whose expected value falls below price. Selling flights through a software optimizer that ignores upsell prompts compresses ancillary yield while raising reservation compute costs.

That buyer-side discipline intersects awkwardly with Washington's scrutiny of seller-side algorithms. In October 2026, the [Federal Trade Commission ordered](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/article/bots-dont-fly-here-united-airlines-restricts-ai-agents-from-booking-flights-203943442.html) United, Delta, JetBlue, and six other carriers to turn over details on how AI tools and personal data influence individualized fares. Carriers want to deploy machine learning internally to segment willingness to pay, while refusing automated software on the buyer's side that automates fare comparison and add-on rejection.

United's contractual wall also exposes an enforcement gap and a sharp divergence across competitors. United explicitly allows AI tools to search and display fares as long as the human clicks buy, yet its revised Rule 1 bars agent bookings routed through travel agencies even though Expedia bookings arrive through agency pipes without flagging whether a person or a browser extension clicked submit. Meanwhile, Southwest launched a ChatGPT discovery plugin in October 2026, and Delta permits authenticated agent transactions with guardrails for sensitive itineraries. Legacy distribution networks such as Amadeus and Travelport are pitching precomputed fare caches and contracted MCP booking feeds precisely to turn "infinite search" into a tollbooth they own.

The strategic equilibrium depends on which side captures customer switching costs first. If Delta and American adopt matching contract bans, personal AI assistants settle into a split workflow where software searches and humans check out, preserving carrier control over the payment screen and upsell ladder. If competing carriers or Visa-backed authenticated agent rails make end-to-end assistant checkout reliable enough to win frequent flyers, United's contractual ban becomes a bargaining posture designed to force AI platforms onto metered, fee-bearing distribution contracts. Until those commercial agreements exist, autonomous travel booking remains confined to APIs and agency rails that carriers can price, throttle, and audit.
