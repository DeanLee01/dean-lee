description: "A DRAM antitrust case moves forward. The court's reasoning shows why high prices alone cannot distinguish scarcity from an agreement."

A company choosing between two products with scarce factory capacity has a reason to favor the more profitable one. When AI customers offer long contracts for high-bandwidth memory, shifting production toward them can make sense even if ordinary memory buyers end up paying more. Three competitors can reach similar decisions without agreeing to do so.

That is part of the defense in a US antitrust case against Samsung, SK hynix and Micron. On November 14, Judge Noël Wise [denied their motion to dismiss](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/11062568/marc-garciaguirre-et-al-v-samsung-electronics-co-ltd-et-al/), allowing the proposed class action to proceed. The plaintiffs allege that the companies agreed to restrict conventional DRAM supply. The ruling is about whether the complaint states a plausible claim, taking well-pleaded allegations as true at this stage. It is not a finding that the companies colluded or violated the law.

The distinction is useful outside a courtroom. A surge in memory prices can arise from stronger demand, slower capacity growth, a product mix change, or a coordinated restriction. Those explanations can produce similar price charts. They imply different things about competition and about how quickly supply might return.

## What the court considered

The complaint alleges that SK hynix announced production cuts in October 2022, Micron followed in November, and Samsung announced cuts in April 2023. The defendants argued that their decisions reflected independent responses to market conditions. The opinion notes that all three had reported substantial losses during the downturn. Cutting production when prices are weak is a coherent commercial response.

The plaintiffs also allege that the companies redirected capacity toward high-bandwidth memory, or HBM, used in AI accelerators. The judge accepted that the pivot could be considered as alleged parallel conduct. But she found that the move toward HBM on its own was insufficient evidence of an agreement: the complaint described HBM as more profitable when the shift began. Nor was a bare decision to restrict supply enough on its own, given that competitors in a concentrated market can respond to the same pressures.

The court found the complaint stronger when it considered other allegations together. These included long-term HBM supply commitments, alleged refusals by Samsung and SK hynix to enter long-term conventional DRAM agreements, and Micron's announced closure of its Crucial consumer brand. The complaint also alleged that the three companies announced plans to wind down mainstream DDR4 production within a few months of one another in 2025. The court treated these as part of a plausible case at the pleading stage; the allegations remain disputed and unproven.

The judge did not accept every proposed inference. The opinion excluded the alleged DDR3 exit from the parallel-conduct analysis because the complaint acknowledged that Micron continued limited DDR3 production. It also said it could not resolve disputes about HBM profitability at this stage, including differences between measures such as price per gigabyte and price per wafer. Those units matter when comparing products that use manufacturing capacity differently.

## The buyer's exposure

The complaint alleges a 697% increase in conventional DRAM prices over an eighteen-month period. That is a claim in the pleadings, not a court-verified index for every memory product. A buyer's actual bill depends on product generation, contract terms, purchase date and the supplier's willingness to fill an order.

The economic exposure is nevertheless straightforward. A PC maker or smaller device business cannot instantly create a new DRAM fab when memory prices rise. If it cannot substitute away from the required part, it must absorb more of the cost, pass it to customers, or change the product it sells. Large buyers may have more negotiating room or longer contracts; the particular terms determine who bears a given increase.

AI customers are competing for manufacturing resources that also support other uses. That does not establish that any one AI order caused a particular retail price increase. Nor does a high price by itself tell us whether producers acted independently. Both claims require a counterfactual: what capacity and prices would have looked like without the demand shift or, separately, without the alleged agreement.

## What evidence would change the view

Internal documents about production plans and capacity constraints could help separate a shared response to market conditions from an agreement. So could evidence about the economics of specific product lines at the time decisions were made. A later price rise alone cannot tell us what a manufacturer knew when it committed capacity years earlier.

For buyers, the immediate lesson is narrower than a prediction about the lawsuit. Memory costs and availability deserve their own assumptions in a hardware budget, especially when a required generation has few substitutes. A dismissal ruling does not add supply or determine damages. The case continues, and the evidence still has to bear the weight of the allegations.
