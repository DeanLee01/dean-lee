When central bankers start tracking your corporate debt issuance alongside energy shocks and sovereign bond volatility, tech capex has officially entered macroeconomics.

On Wednesday, the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee published the record of its quarterly policy meeting. Alongside renewed inflation risks from oil and gas disruptions pushing benchmark sovereign yields to heights unseen since 2008, the Committee singled out a newer transmission channel. The rapid increase in artificial intelligence-related debt issuance, the FPC warned, broadens the exposure of global capital markets to operational shocks and valuation swings in frontier AI, raising the odds that multiple balance-sheet vulnerabilities crystallize simultaneously.

The conventional defense of the current compute buildout relies on private balance sheets. Tech hyperscalers, the argument goes, entered this cycle with historic cash reserves and negligible leverage. If model labs overbuild or software monetization lags hardware depreciation schedules, equity holders absorb the write-downs. Under that view, an oversupplied cluster of accelerators poses an equity valuation problem, not a systemic credit event.

That framing ignores where the marginal dollar of infrastructure financing now comes from.

As annual capital expenditures across the primary cloud providers crossed the hundred-billion-dollar mark, the funding stack underwent a quiet structural migration. Tier-one technology firms still fund initial model development from internal cash flow, but the utility-scale infrastructure surrounding it (dedicated power substations, gigawatt-scale data center campuses, private transmission lines, and bespoke liquid-cooling facilities) increasingly relies on debt.

Hyperscalers issue investment-grade corporate bonds to fund buildouts. Private credit funds underwrite asset-backed loans against server clusters whose secondary liquidation value hinges on rapidly depreciating silicon. Real estate developers and independent power producers tap leveraged loan markets to construct purpose-built facilities backed by long-dated hyperscaler leases.

In isolation, each of these credit tranches looks sound. Credit analysts underwrite the loans against investment-grade corporate guarantees and visible contract backstops. But debt markets are not isolated from sovereign term premia.

When the macro environment shifts (when persistent commodity pressures keep inflation sticky and governments flood primary dealer desks with sovereign paper), the discount rate applied to thirty-year infrastructure cash flows resets upward. A debt-financed data center built on a five-year payback assumption cannot easily absorb an unhedged rise in funding costs if API token pricing simultaneously commoditizes.

Governor Andrew Bailey made an unusual accompanying intervention on Wednesday, arguing that while risks from frontier AI are growing, formal statutory regulation is not the right place to start. His case was straightforward: before governments attempt to write rigid statutory codes, market supervisors must understand the mechanical failure points and establish concrete intervention triggers through rigorous pre- and post-deployment stress testing.

Bailey was speaking primarily about algorithmic safety and operational resilience. Yet the exact same principle applies to capital structure.

Statutory hand-wringing over whether AI represents an asset bubble misses how credit cycles work. Bubbles financed entirely by common equity deflate quietly through compressed multiples. Bubbles financed through leveraged debt structures, credit revolvers, and yield-seeking private credit vehicles deflate through margin calls, covenant breaches, and liquidity fire-sales.

When an infrastructure buildout is capitalized with fixed debt obligations, its solvency requires revenue velocity to exceed the carrying cost of the capital. If frontier model advances hit diminishing commercial returns while corporate bond coupons reset at fifteen-year highs, the risk does not remain quarantined inside Silicon Valley. It sits on the balance sheets of the pension funds, commercial insurers, and debt syndicates that bought the paper to begin with.
