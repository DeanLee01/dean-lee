White House National Economic Council Director Kevin Hassett appeared on CNBC following a sharp technology selloff that pushed the Nasdaq Composite to multi-month lows. Asked about the sustainability of debt and capital expenditure in the artificial intelligence buildout, Hassett dismissed systemic risk concerns. He described the sector as a well-financed ecosystem supported by large corporate balance sheets, noting that well-capitalized corporations are positioned across the market, holding equity and debt and ready to step in if smaller participants stumble.

His premise on capital availability is technically accurate. The four primary hyperscalers collectively maintain hundreds of billions of dollars in cash, short-term marketable securities, and operating cash flow generated by core enterprise software, cloud hosting, and digital advertising franchises. Investment-grade bond markets routinely oversubscribe their multi-billion-dollar debt issuances within hours. Meanwhile, private credit funds and institutional asset managers have assembled dedicated hundred-billion-dollar infrastructure pools to underwrite physical campuses, backup generators, and transmission substations. Wholesale capital markets have not closed their doors to artificial intelligence operators.

Abundant liquidity does not establish economic solvency. Readily available funding often sustains negative-present-value projects until amortization schedules collide with revenue realities. The physical economics of artificial intelligence compute impose a severe duration mismatch that distinguishes this capital buildout from earlier utility cycles.

Traditional infrastructure assets like fiber optic conduits, toll bridges, and natural gas pipelines amortize across twenty to forty years. A conduit laid in the ground retains utility even if traffic develops slowly over a decade. An artificial intelligence accelerator faces an effective economic lifespan of thirty-six to forty-eight months. When newer chip generations deliver three to four times more inference tokens per megawatt-hour, older clusters experience rapid economic obsolescence. An operator cannot simply wait for future demand to materialize while holding older hardware, because fixed power contracts, cooling overhead, and facility carry costs quickly exceed the market clearing price of the tokens those older chips produce.

The financing structure of the buildout has also evolved. Early cluster deployments were funded almost entirely out of uncommitted corporate free cash flow. With annual hyperscaler capital expenditures pacing toward 300 billion dollars, funding has expanded into balance-sheet debt, project-level financing, and private credit facilities secured against server racks. Several neo-cloud operators and specialized hosting providers borrow at high single-digit or low double-digit coupons to finance hardware that depreciates straight-line at twenty-five to thirty percent per year. When fixed debt service attaches to hardware with rapid obsolescence, baseline operational margins leave little room for utilization shocks.

Hassett suggested that deep-pocketed corporations stand ready to absorb debt and equity if weaker operators struggle. That dynamic describes a distressed asset auction rather than sustainable return on invested capital. If an over-leveraged datacenter venture breaches its debt covenants, a hyperscaler or distressed debt vehicle can purchase the underlying hardware at thirty cents on the dollar. That outcome may prevent disorderly contagion across the banking system, but it records an irreversible destruction of invested equity.

For engineering teams and enterprise technology buyers, the consequence appears in contract terms. The period of venture-subsidized compute tokens was an artifact of hyperscalers treating hardware buildouts as land-grab customer acquisition. As debt service schedules and accelerated depreciation hit corporate income statements, compute providers must enforce capital recovery. Multi-year reserved capacity agreements are requiring higher minimum commitments, baseline token pricing floors are firming up, and pipeline architectural efficiency now dictates software margins far more than marginal gains on public model benchmarks.
