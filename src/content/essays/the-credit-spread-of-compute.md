Meta Platforms lifted the lower bound of its 2026 capital expenditure guidance to between $135 billion and $145 billion on October 22, signaling that operating cash flow alone may no longer cover the build-out. Alongside the revised spending range, discussions of fresh equity offerings and multi-tranche bond sales triggered an immediate selloff. Meta shares dropped seven percent, but the cleaner pricing signal showed up in the credit market: five-year credit default swaps on Meta widened 7.6 basis points in a single session to 114.79 basis points, an all-time high.

For the past three years, public equity markets treated AI infrastructure as an unhedged call option funded entirely by software rents. Digital advertising, cloud margins, and enterprise subscriptions produced predictable cash streams that absorbed escalating GPU procurement without stressing corporate balance sheets. In that regime, capital expenditure was an equity story. Investors debated terminal valuation multiples, software monetization timelines, and market share capture. The balance sheet remained invisible because free cash flow remained positive.

That insulation thins once annual capital spending crosses the hundred-billion-dollar threshold. When a company with fifty billion dollars in baseline operating cash flow commits to spending nearly triple that amount across compute clusters, high-voltage substations, and long-term power purchase agreements, the funding structure changes. The gap between cash from operations and cash spent on infrastructure must be bridged with external liabilities. Tech giants that operated for a decade as net-cash fortresses are turning into industrial debt issuers.

The corporate bond market operates on different mathematics than equity research desks. Equity investors price optionality, narrative reach, and upside skew. Bondholders price duration, coverage ratios, and recovery value in distress. When a software company buys custom silicon and builds multi-gigawatt facilities, it trades liquid cash for physical assets that face rapid technological depreciation. An accelerator cluster carries an economic life of three to five years before newer architectures render its power-per-flop uncompetitive. Debt issued to finance that cluster carries a ten-year or thirty-year maturity.

The tension lies in that duration mismatch. If a tech firm finances a five-year depreciating asset with thirty-year paper, the debt outlives the silicon. By the time the bonds mature, the original data center has been re-architected or decommissioned, while the coupon obligations remain fixed on the ledger. 

The bull case for balance-sheet expansion rests on competitive foreclosure. In a market where model capability scales with cluster size, under-investing guarantees obsolescence. If spending $140 billion secures the dominant platform for consumer agents and enterprise workflow, paying an extra eighty basis points on senior notes or diluting equity by two percent is a modest premium for survival. Meta's advertising engine continues to generate substantial operating gross margin, providing an underlying cushion that speculative infrastructure startups lack.

Yet credit markets price the left tail rather than the median outcome. Widening default swaps and rising risk premiums on mega-cap tech bonds indicate that fixed-income investors are applying a utility discount to technology balance sheets. As borrowing volumes surge to fund multi-year compute commitments, the cost of capital is no longer an abstract hurdle rate chosen by corporate treasury. It is an active constraint quoted daily on credit desks, where every basis point of spread demands higher operating returns from models that have yet to prove their unit economics.
